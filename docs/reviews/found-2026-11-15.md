# Found on 15 November 2026, fixed in Nib 1.7.1

Two faults found while the worker pool was being prepared (`docs/plans/worker-pool.md`). Both were in 1.6.7 and in
1.7.0, and neither had to do with the speed work. Croix had both fixed the same day ("Go ahead in that order": the
faults as 1.7.1, then the pool). Each has a probe in `tools/verify/` that exits 1 while its fault is there.

1. A page got eight joins and no more.
2. A symbol whose strokes were moved apart was still read as what it had been.

# 1. A page got eight joins and no more

## What happened

A board reads its writing as pieces (the clusters `clustersOf` finds), then joins pieces that belong together but were
left apart by the space between them: a line that only asks (`x =`) under an equation, and the parts of one line with
a wide gap in it (`joinOperands`, `src/pad.js`). The read asked `joinOperands` again after each join, "at most a few
rounds":

```js
for (let round = 0; round < 8; round++) {          // _partsPass: the scheduled read and recognize() both run it
  const joined = joinOperands(parts); if (!joined) break;
  …
```

but `joinOperands` returns as soon as it has made **one** join. So a page got eight joins a read, in a fixed order,
and the pairs after the eighth were never joined, however often the page was read.

**What that cost, and what it did not.** The pairs left apart stayed two pieces: each half read by itself (without
the other half to read it by), two lines of the page's reading and of its LaTeX, selected, moved and tidied apart.
It did **not** cost answers, which is what this note first said: when the pad works out answers it finds a sum's other
half and an `x =`'s equation by where they stand (`_replan`: "writing carried on past a gap on the same line is one
problem", "an x = may also solve an equation in the writing just above it"), joined or not. The probe counts the
answers for that reason, and they are the same before and after.

It took a crowded page. Up to eight such pairs nothing was different, and writing that sits close enough is one
cluster to begin with and needs no join.

## How to see it

`node tools/verify/joins_probe.js [sizes] [engine]` builds pages from the school sample and, after the pad has read
each, asks `joinOperands` what it would still join:

| Page | Clusters | 1.7.0: joined, left undone | 1.7.1: joined, left undone | Answered as when read alone (both) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 12 sums with a gap each | 24 | 8, 3 | 11, 0 | 11 of 12 |
| 20 sums with a gap each | 40 | 8, 11 | 19, 0 | 19 of 20 |
| 40 sums with a gap each | 80 | 8, 31 | 39, 0 | 39 of 40 |
| 12 equations, each asked `letter =` afterwards | 19 | 6, 0 | 6, 0 | 10 of 12 |
| 20 equations, each asked `letter =` afterwards | 32 | 8, 3 | 11, 0 | 16 of 20 |
| 40 equations, each asked `letter =` afterwards | 52 | 8, 8 | 16, 0 | 23 of 40 |

(The one sum in each page that is not answered as alone is split before a minus sign, "33.40      − 7.9 =", which the
rule does not join: a piece may begin with a negative number. The equations not answered are the probe's own: its
`letter =` lines are made by moving strokes, and some are misread or land in the piece beside them.)

## The fix (1.7.1)

Every join makes one piece out of two, so as many rounds as there are pieces is every join there can be: the bound
is the number of pieces. Three things come with it, all in `_partsPass`:

- **A limit that is counted, not timed.** `joinOperands` looks at every pair of pieces each time it is asked (11 ns a
  pair), so a page of thousands of pieces with thousands of joins would take minutes. The rounds are
  `max(8, min(pieces, floor(JOIN_WORK / pieces²)))` with `JOIN_WORK` = 30 million pairs (a third of a second here):
  the number of pieces up to 310, fewer above (120 at 500 pieces, 30 at 1,000), never under the eight there were.
  Counted so that what a page reads does not depend on the machine. (The pairs are all it counts: reading each
  joined piece comes on top, as it always did.)
- **Each round is a step.** A round whose joined piece had been read before had no step of its own, so many of them
  ran with the page held; each now ends in a `yield`.
- **What a pass has read stays read for the pass.** `this.parts` keeps 300 readings and lets go of all of them when it
  has more; on a page of more than 300 pieces every round then read the dropped ones again (the test's page of 400
  pieces: 6.4 s, 1.4 s with this). The pass keeps its own (`got`); unreadable pieces are not in it, so they are
  counted round by round as before.

Readings on pages of eight joins or fewer are what they were: the scripted sessions' 640 steps and the 51-step
fingerprint are unchanged.

**Left as it was**: a join that takes in writing that cannot be read (a read that throws) still ends the joining for
that read (`if (bad > was) break`), so the pairs after it stay apart. It takes an engine fault to get there.

# 2. A symbol whose strokes were moved apart was still read as what it had been

## What happened

A recognizer keeps what it has classified: each group of strokes under the strokes' ids and the size it was read at
(`Recognizer.cache`), and each pair of neighboring strokes' chance of being one symbol under their ids
(`Recognizer.jcache`). A stroke kept its id for as long as it was the same object, and the select tool moves a stroke
in place. `Pad._inkMoved` clears the pad's readings so the ink is read again — "ink that moved is read again where it
is" — but only ink that was made bigger or smaller got new recognizers. So when **one stroke of a symbol was moved
and the other was not**, the pair was still in the cache as it had been before the move, and the read that followed
found it there. Undo of such a move, and a host moving one of its strokes (`syncStrokes`), went the same way.

Ink moved as a whole (a selection of whole expressions, which is what moving is mostly used for) was never touched by
this: its strokes keep their places relative to each other, and the old classification is still the right one.

## How to see it

`node tools/verify/moved_probe.js [engine]`: "2 + 3" with the plus written as two strokes; the bar of the plus is
selected and nudged; the pad reads again, and a recognizer that has never seen the strokes reads the same ink.

| The bar of the plus | 1.7.0 reads | 1.7.1 reads, and so does a recognizer new to the ink |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nudged down by 26 | `2+3` | `21-3` |
| nudged down by 40 (clear of the stroke it crossed) | `2+3` | `213` over `-` |
| nudged up by 30 | `2+3` | `2T3` |

The same on a board and on a pad of one expression. It lasted until the page was opened again or the ink resized.
The case that would be met in a lesson: two bars written a little too far apart to be an equals sign, one of them
dragged closer — the pair had been classified as it stood, and stayed so. Over the school problems, with one stroke
or the tail of an expression moved at random, 1.7.0 gave a stale reading after 98 of 318 moves
(`test/reading_check.js`, section 3, run against 1.7.0).

## The fix (1.7.1)

The recognizer itself now sees that a stroke is not where it was (`Recognizer.idsOf`, `src/recognize.js`); the pad
does nothing new, so every way ink is moved in place is covered — a drag, the arrow keys, Undo and Redo, a host's
`syncStrokes`, a host that hands `Nib.recognize` the same arrays moved. With each stroke it keeps the stroke's shape
(the number of points, its box's size, the way from its first point to its last, and three sums over its points, two
of them weighted by each point's place so that the same points in another order are another shape) and where its
first point was when it got its id. Asked again:

- a stroke whose shape is another gets a new id;
- strokes that have all moved alike — a piece dragged as a whole, or nothing moved — keep their ids, so **nothing is
  classified again** and every reading is bit for bit what it was (the sessions and the fingerprints agree);
- when they have not moved alike, those that moved as most did keep their ids and the rest get new ones: the groups
  and pairs with a moved stroke in them are classified again, the rest of the piece is not.

"Alike" is to within 512 steps of the floating-point grid at the strokes' coordinates: moving a stroke by w and
taking the difference does not give w back to the last bit.

An independent review (an Opus agent, given the diff, twice) found four holes, all closed before release and all
pinned in `test/reading_check.js`. In the first version: the tolerance was a share of the largest coordinate in the
whole input, so small writing far from the origin — or any writing with one stroke far away — kept ids through real
moves; and the shape went by sums that do not change when a stroke's points are put in another order. In the
second: the two weights rose evenly with a point's place in the stroke, which lets a bend of four neighboring points
through (one up, two down, one up), so they are now the place's bits mixed; and a stroke first read beside a piece
that had been read far away and brought home was given a new id on every read (its place was kept more coarsely
than it was compared), which wasted work without ever reading wrongly. The review also tried what must not happen
the other way — ids lost by ink that only moved as a whole: drags, 500 arrow-key nudges, moves of a ten-millionth
and of a hundred million, at coordinates up to 900 million, strokes of 5,000 points — and every id was kept.

**Known and left**: a stroke read in turn with two sets of strokes that moved differently (a host reading
overlapping parts of its ink through one recognizer) is given a new id each time — classified more often than it
need be, never read as what it no longer is. On a pad the sets a recognizer is given are one inside the other (a
piece, then the joined pieces that hold it), which settles after one read.
